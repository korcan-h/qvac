The history of the printing press begins in the fifteenth century, when Johannes Gutenberg combined movable metal type, oil-based ink and a wooden screw press into a system that could produce books far faster than scribes. Within fifty years presses were running in more than two hundred cities across Europe, and the number of books in circulation grew from thousands to millions. Cheaper books spread literacy, helped standardise languages and spelling, and allowed scientific results to be checked and built upon by readers who would never meet the authors. Historians still debate how much of the Reformation, the scientific revolution and the rise of newspapers depended on this one invention, but few doubt that it changed how knowledge moved between people. Later improvements, from steam-powered presses to rotary printing and offset lithography, kept lowering the cost of a printed page until mass-market paperbacks and daily papers became ordinary parts of life.
